What makes a comic worth something?

Four things decide most of it: whether the book is a key, what shape it's in, how many good copies exist, and how many people want one right now. Here's each in plain English.

Key issues

A key is an issue that matters to the story or to collectors: a character's first appearance, an origin, a death, a first issue, or a famous cover. Keys are what most collectors chase, so they carry most of the value. A first appearance in rough shape often beats a common issue in great shape.

Not every key is obvious. Villains and side characters who later star in movies and TV shows can turn a forgotten issue into a hot one almost overnight.

Eras

Collectors split comics into ages. The dates are rough and people argue about the edges:

  • Golden Age (about 1938 to 1956): Superman, Batman, Wonder Woman and Captain America begin. Few copies survived the paper drives and the trash.
  • Silver Age (about 1956 to 1970): the Flash returns and Marvel's heroes arrive: Spider-Man, the Fantastic Four, the X-Men, the Hulk.
  • Bronze Age (about 1970 to 1985): darker stories and a wave of new characters such as Wolverine, the Punisher and Ghost Rider.
  • Copper and Modern (mid-'80s on): huge print runs in the '90s mean most books from that time are common, with a handful of exceptions.

Older isn't automatically valuable, but the older the book, the fewer nice copies are left.

Condition and grading

Condition is graded on a 10-point scale, and it moves price more than anything except key status. The same issue can be worth a few dollars in one grade and thousands in another. Tap a grade to see what it means:

Graders look at the spine, corners, creases, tears, color, gloss, staples and the paper itself. Pages that have turned brown or brittle pull a grade down even when the cover looks good.

Raw or slabbed

A raw comic is just a comic. A slabbed comic has been graded by a company such as CGC or CBCS and sealed in a hard plastic case with a label showing the grade. A trusted grade makes a book easier to sell for full value, especially an expensive one.

CGC's label color tells you something too: blue is a standard grade, yellow means it was signed in front of a witness, purple means it has been restored, and green means a qualified grade (for example a missing coupon or an unwitnessed signature). Restoration and qualified grades usually sell for less.

Grading costs money and takes time, so it only makes sense for books worth enough to cover it. You don't need to grade anything before selling to me.

Variants, printings and reprints

  • Price variants: some issues were printed with a different cover price for a test market, like the famous 35-cent copies of Marvel's 1977 Star Wars #1. They can be worth far more than the regular copy.
  • Newsstand and direct editions: from the '80s on, copies sold in comic shops and copies sold on newsstands can have different barcode boxes. For some issues one version is much scarcer.
  • Reprints and facsimiles: famous issues get reprinted, sometimes with the original cover. A facsimile edition looks like the real thing but shows a modern cover price and says so inside. It's a fun read, not the original.
  • Later printings: a second or third printing is almost always worth less than the first.

Why prices move

Comics are a market, and markets move. A movie or TV announcement can send a book up fast, and the excitement can fade just as fast. Prices for high-grade copies of the same issue can swing more than low-grade ones, and a few big sales can make a book look hotter than it is.

That's why there's no price list on this site. A number from last year can be badly wrong today, in either direction. The only honest price is one based on your actual copy and what that grade is selling for now.

How to check a value yourself

Look at what books sold for, not what people are asking:

  • eBay sold listings: search the title and issue number, then turn on the "Sold items" filter. Match the grade or condition as closely as you can.
  • Heritage Auctions past results at ha.com: real auction prices for graded books, free with an account.
  • Price-tracking sites such as GoCollect and GPA collect recent graded sales; some features need a subscription.

Expect a buyer who resells to offer less than full retail; that's how any dealer stays in business. What you should expect is a clear explanation of how the number was reached.

Storing what you keep

  • Use bags and backing boards made for comics, and store books standing upright in a comic box.
  • Keep them cool, dry and dark. Sunlight, attic heat and garage humidity do real damage.
  • No tape, rubber bands, paper clips or stacking heavy things on top.
  • Handle old books with clean, dry hands, and support the whole book when you lift it.
